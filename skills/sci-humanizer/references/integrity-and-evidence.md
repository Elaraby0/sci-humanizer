# Integrity, evidence, and statistical language

Use this reference whenever the text contains scientific claims, citations,
statistics, confidential material, ethics statements, or publication decisions.

## 1. Build a claim ledger before rewriting

For each substantive sentence, identify:

- the claim;
- its source or data support;
- the population, setting, exposure, intervention, comparison, and outcome;
- the design and analysis that permit the inference;
- the direction and magnitude of the result;
- the uncertainty or limitation; and
- whether the statement is observation, interpretation, speculation, or
  recommendation.

Preserve this ledger through revision. A smoother sentence is wrong if it
changes any of these elements.

## 2. Calibrate verbs to evidence

Choose a verb that matches what the design supports.

### Descriptive evidence

Use verbs such as `reported`, `observed`, `measured`, `estimated`, `described`,
or `was more common`.

### Associational evidence

Use `was associated with`, `correlated with`, `co-occurred with`, `predicted`
only in its statistical sense, or `participants with X had higher Y`.

### Causal evidence

Use `caused`, `reduced`, `prevented`, `improved`, or `led to` only when the
design, conduct, and analysis justify a causal interpretation. Randomization
strengthens causal inference but does not erase nonadherence, attrition,
measurement error, missingness, multiplicity, or external-validity limits.

### Mechanisms

Distinguish demonstrated mediation or mechanism from plausible explanation.
Use `may reflect`, `could arise from`, or `one explanation is` for inference,
but do not stack qualifiers until the sentence becomes meaningless.

## 3. Report quantities transparently

Where relevant, include:

- numerator and denominator;
- group-specific values;
- effect size and direction;
- confidence or credible interval;
- time horizon;
- measurement units;
- sample size used for that analysis;
- missing data or attrition; and
- whether an analysis was primary, secondary, exploratory, adjusted, or
  post hoc.

Do not treat a P value as an effect size or a binary truth test. Reserve
`statistically significant` for the prespecified statistical criterion and
avoid using `significant` as a synonym for `important` nearby.

Do not infer equivalence or no effect merely because a result is not
statistically significant. State the estimate and uncertainty. Use
`inconclusive`, `compatible with`, or a design-specific equivalence statement
when justified.

Use decimal precision consistent with measurement and analytic precision. Keep
units and denominators consistent across text, tables, figures, and abstract.

## 4. Present risk for non-specialists

Whenever possible, provide both baseline and comparison risks on the same
denominator and time scale.

Example pattern:

> Risk increased from 30 to 38 cases per 10,000 people per year, an absolute
> increase of 8 per 10,000 and a relative increase of about 27%.

Do not report the relative increase alone when it makes a small baseline risk
sound large. Do not report only the absolute change when population burden or a
large relative difference is important. Give both when space permits.

## 5. Separate reporting from interpretation

- Methods: what was planned or done and why.
- Results: what was found.
- Discussion: what the findings may mean.
- Recommendation: what someone should do, supported by the applicable evidence
  and values.

Do not put an analytic rationale into Results merely because it is adjacent to
the output. Do not write a speculative mechanism as a result. Do not turn a
discussion inference into a factual abstract conclusion.

## 6. Write limitations that inform interpretation

A useful limitation statement answers four questions:

1. What feature of the design, data, or analysis is limited?
2. What inference could it distort or prevent?
3. What direction or magnitude of distortion is plausible, if known?
4. What mitigation was attempted, and what uncertainty remains?

Avoid generic lists such as `small sample size, possible bias, and limited
generalizability` without explaining their consequences. Do not use a
limitations paragraph to dismiss every concern. A sensitivity analysis may
reduce a concern without eliminating it.

## 7. Preserve negative evidence

Report null, negative, contradictory, and adverse findings when they bear on
the question. Do not selectively humanize favorable results while compressing
or omitting inconvenient ones. Distinguish absence of evidence from evidence of
absence.

## 8. Handle citations safely

- Never invent a citation, DOI, PMID, page number, author, journal, or quotation.
- Keep citations attached to the claims they support.
- Prefer the primary source for a specific result, method, or quotation.
- Verify a secondary source's citation chain before reusing it.
- If a citation appears mismatched, flag it rather than silently moving or
  replacing it.
- Do not cite a review as though it conducted the primary experiment unless the
  sentence is explicitly about the review's synthesis.
- Preserve citation syntax and reference-manager fields in file edits.

When current verification is requested or necessary, use authoritative
databases and primary literature. State clearly when a conclusion is an
inference from sources rather than a directly reported result.

## 9. Avoid plagiarism and deceptive paraphrase

Plagiarism includes copying a sentence or distinctive phrase without quotation
and attribution, rearranging another author's words, mimicking the sentence
structure while substituting synonyms, and presenting another author's
synthesis or citation chain as one's own work.

Safe synthesis process:

1. Read and understand the source.
2. Record the claim, evidence, and citation separately from the source wording.
3. Draft from that understanding without looking line by line at the original.
4. Compare the draft with the source for accidental phrase overlap.
5. Quote and cite language that must remain exact.

Text recycling from the author's prior work may still violate venue policy or
copyright. Methods language can sometimes resemble prior descriptions, but the
current journal's policy governs. Do not conceal duplicate or overlapping
publication.

## 10. Authorship and contributorship

Do not infer authorship from seniority, funding, supervision, editing, or
prestige alone. For biomedical work, consult current ICMJE criteria and the
venue policy. Discuss authorship early, revisit it as contributions change, and
record specific contributions. CRediT can describe contributor roles but does
not itself decide who qualifies as an author.

Never list an AI system as an author. Humans must review and accept
responsibility for all submitted content.

## 11. AI-assisted writing disclosure

Do not promise secrecy or detector evasion. Follow the target venue, funder, or
institution's current disclosure requirements. Current ICMJE guidance requires
transparent disclosure of AI-assisted use and holds human authors responsible
for accuracy, originality, attribution, and permissions.

Do not upload a confidential submitted manuscript to an external AI service
unless the user has authority, gives informed permission, and the journal or
institution permits it.

## 12. Inclusive and accurate population language

Describe populations with the terms the study actually measured. Do not
silently convert sex to gender, race to ancestry, nationality to ethnicity, or
identity to biology. State how categories were defined and collected when
relevant. Prefer community-accepted and person-centered terms unless a
population explicitly prefers identity-first language or the scientific
construct requires another usage.

Avoid treating socially constructed categories as biological mechanisms. Do
not generalize beyond represented populations.

## 13. Integrity audit before final output

Ask:

- Did any number, denominator, unit, date, or sample size change?
- Did an association become causal?
- Did uncertainty disappear or multiply without reason?
- Did a null or adverse finding vanish?
- Did a limitation become boilerplate or get falsely “resolved”?
- Did a citation move away from its claim or appear without verification?
- Did the rewrite add an author, contribution, ethics approval, registration,
  funding statement, or disclosure not in the source?
- Does the abstract still match the main text and displays?
- Would a domain expert recognize the same scientific claim after revision?

If any answer raises doubt, restore the supported meaning and flag the issue to
the user.
