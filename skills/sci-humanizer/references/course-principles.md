# Course principles for clear scientific writing

Use this reference for drafting, substantive editing, or explaining scientific
writing decisions. These principles synthesize Modules 1 through 4 of the local
Stanford *Writing in the Sciences* corpus.

## 1. Start with meaning

The purpose of scientific writing is to communicate an idea clearly and
effectively. Elegant prose is a product of revision, not a requirement for the
first draft. Before editing a confusing paragraph, ask what the paragraph is
trying to say. If that cannot be stated in one sentence, the problem is likely
conceptual or structural rather than lexical.

Useful questions:

- What is the one take-home message?
- Why does this reader need it here?
- What evidence supports it?
- What is the simplest accurate way to state it?
- Which details are essential for interpretation, evaluation, or replication?

Complex science does not require needlessly complex language. Aim for prose
that a qualified reader can understand on the first careful reading.

## 2. Cut clutter

Strip sentences to the words that carry meaning. Cut a word or phrase when its
removal changes neither the proposition nor a necessary shade of uncertainty.

### Common sources of clutter

- Empty openings: `It is important to note that`, `It should be emphasized
  that`, `It is well known that`, `In the present study` when the context
  already establishes the study.
- Long equivalents: `in order to` -> `to`; `a large number of` -> `many`;
  `has the ability to` -> `can`; `due to the fact that` -> `because`.
- Redundant pairs: `illustrate and demonstrate`, `challenges and difficulties`,
  `basic fundamentals`, `future plans`.
- Empty intensifiers: `very`, `really`, `quite`, `basically`, `generally` when
  they do not quantify or qualify meaning.
- Avoidable negatives: `did not remember` -> `forgot`; `not important` ->
  `unimportant`, provided the revision preserves nuance.
- Empty existential syntax: replace unnecessary `there is/are/was/were` with a
  concrete subject.
- Needless prepositions and nominal phrases: `protective for the occurrence of`
  -> `may protect against`.
- Repetition disguised by a thesaurus. If a writer reaches for a synonym merely
  to avoid repeating a technical keyword, first ask whether the second sentence
  is needed. If both instances are needed, repeat the precise keyword.

### What not to cut

Do not remove a qualifier that expresses real uncertainty, a denominator, a
unit, a comparison group, an eligibility condition, a method needed for
replication, a limitation that affects interpretation, or a transition that
encodes a genuine logical relationship.

The goal is concision, not minimum word count.

## 3. Write with verbs

Verbs move sentences. Nominalizations often hide the action, add helper verbs,
and push the main point to the end.

Prefer:

- `we analyzed` over `we conducted an analysis of`;
- `cells responded` over `a response of the cells was observed`;
- `depleting calcium activates the channels` over `activation of the channels
  is induced by depletion of calcium`.

Do not convert every technical noun into a verb. Established entities such as
`randomization`, `transcription`, or `regression` may be the correct scientific
term. Flag nominalizations when they weaken syntax, not merely because of their
suffix.

### Keep subject and verb close

A long interruption between the grammatical subject and main verb makes the
reader retain too much unresolved information. Move conditions or definitions
before the subject, after the main clause, or into another sentence when doing
so clarifies the action.

## 4. Choose voice deliberately

Active voice usually improves readability, identifies responsibility, and
reveals ambiguity. Ask `Who did what to whom?`

Prefer active voice when:

- the actor made a scientific or analytic choice;
- responsibility matters;
- the passive hides the causal sequence;
- naming the actor shortens the sentence; or
- the prose belongs to the introduction, results, or discussion and the active
  version reads naturally.

Passive voice is useful when:

- the object or procedure deserves emphasis;
- the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or already obvious;
- a methods description would become a monotonous chain of `we` sentences; or
- information flow requires the old information to remain in subject position.

The course favors active voice but does not treat passive voice as an error.
Use either purposefully.

### First person

`I` and `we` are compatible with formal scientific writing when the venue
permits them. They can honestly identify who designed, measured, analyzed, or
interpreted the work. Removing the researchers does not make the research more
objective.

## 5. Make syntax parallel

Pairs joined by `and`, `or`, or `but` should match grammatically. Items in a
list should use the same form.

Nonparallel:

> The study aimed to measure exposure, estimating risk, and to identify
> modifiers.

Parallel:

> The study aimed to measure exposure, estimate risk, and identify modifiers.

Parallelism clarifies relationships and can add rhythm. Do not force unrelated
ideas into a list merely to produce a polished cadence.

## 6. Use punctuation to expose relationships

The course encourages writers to use more than commas and periods.

- Use a colon after a complete clause to introduce an explanation, list,
  example, or consequence.
- Use a semicolon to join closely related independent clauses or to separate
  complex list items.
- Use parentheses for information that is genuinely secondary.
- Use a dash to frame an interruption or place emphasis on what follows.

Punctuation must serve the argument. An em dash is permitted in SciHumanizer,
despite the general Humanizer's default ban. Match a supplied venue or author
sample and avoid turning every sentence into a dramatic aside.

## 7. Treat the paragraph as the unit of composition

Each paragraph should perform one main function. Scientific paragraphs often
become difficult because they contain several unrelated ideas or postpone the
point until after a pile of details.

### Paragraph design

1. State or strongly signal the main point early.
2. Develop it with evidence, reasoning, examples, or necessary qualification.
3. End on a useful implication, contrast, or bridge when the argument needs one.

This is not a demand that every paragraph begin with a formulaic topic
sentence. Vary the form while keeping the function clear.

### Logical paths readers can follow

- general -> specific;
- known -> unknown;
- question -> answer;
- cause -> effect;
- problem -> response;
- earlier -> later;
- claim -> evidence -> qualification.

Group like ideas. If the same topic appears in distant paragraphs, move the
material together and remove resulting repetition. When presenting a
controversy, avoid repeated `argument/counterargument/rebuttal` whiplash. A
clearer order may group the main case, the countercase, and the response.

### Transitions

Sound logic produces flow. A transition word cannot repair a broken argument.
Prefer ordinary connectors such as `but` and `and` when they express the
relationship. Avoid a paragraph in which every sentence begins with `however`,
`furthermore`, `therefore`, or another signpost.

### Paragraph length

Shorter paragraphs improve focus and provide white space, but there is no
universal sentence count. Split a paragraph when its function changes, not
merely because a checker reports a threshold.

## 8. Separate writing stages

The course distinguishes three stages.

### Prewriting

Spend substantial effort collecting, synthesizing, and organizing material.
Create a road map showing the purpose of each section and paragraph. Put facts,
quotes, citations, and data at hand before composing. Do not alternate every
sentence with a new literature search if the needed evidence could have been
gathered first.

### First draft

Write complete sentences in the planned order. Focus on the high-level message
and logic. Do not demand polished sentences from the first draft. Drafting and
line editing at the same time slows composition and can obscure the overall
argument.

### Revision

Revision is where clarity and style emerge. Read the text aloud. Review the
organization before polishing sentences. Run a verb check, cut ruthlessly,
inspect jargon and acronyms, and seek an outside reader when possible. Ask that
reader to state the main findings, significance, and take-home message.

Before submission, check numerical consistency across text, tables, figures,
and abstract. Trace citations to primary sources and eliminate references that
do not support the attached claim.

## 9. Human scientific style

Scientific prose can be lively without becoming promotional. Use precise
images, a concrete example, or a memorable turn when the genre allows it. Take
stylistic risks only when they help readers understand or care about the work.

Natural prose varies sentence length and structure. It does not require fake
candor, dramatic fragments, ornamental metaphors, forced triplets, or vague
claims of importance. Preserve the author's legitimate uncertainty, humor,
specific details, and informed judgment.

## 10. Resolve course advice contextually

Several course rules are conditional:

- Active voice is the default, not an absolute.
- Jargon is minimized for general readers but may be required in methods.
- Repetition is cut when it duplicates meaning but retained for stable
  technical keywords and central findings.
- Details are filtered for public audiences but retained for replication and
  critical appraisal in scientific manuscripts.
- Paragraphs are kept focused and readable, not forced into an arbitrary size.
- The abstract is written last for a completed manuscript, but a conference
  abstract may necessarily precede the final paper and must then be rebuilt
  from the finalized analysis rather than recycled uncritically.
