# Genre guides

Use the relevant section of this reference rather than loading every genre into
the response. Specific venue, funder, rubric, or user instructions override
these defaults.

## Research manuscript workflow

For a completed study, the course recommends drafting in this order:

1. finalize tables and figures;
2. write Results;
3. write Methods;
4. write Introduction;
5. write Discussion; and
6. write the Abstract last.

This order treats the data display as the foundation of the paper's story. It
is guidance, not a reason to disrupt a productive user workflow.

## Tables and figures

Tables and figures should be understandable without repeatedly consulting the
main text.

- Give each item one clear purpose and a concise, informative title.
- Define abbreviations, symbols, units, measurement conventions, and essential
  experimental details in the title, headings, legend, or footnotes.
- Use consistent keywords in titles, labels, legends, and prose.
- Use the fewest items needed to tell the story. Do not show the same data in
  both a table and a figure without a reason.
- Use figures for visual patterns, trends, distributions, primary evidence,
  workflows, or a single result with visual impact.
- Use tables when readers need exact values or many variables.
- Match the target venue's current specifications instead of treating any
  course-era formatting convention as universal.
- Report units and only defensible precision. Extra decimal places imply
  measurement precision the study may not have.
- In a statistical footnote, state the groups compared, the test used, and what
  each symbol means.
- Consider flow diagrams, study schematics, and conceptual diagrams when prose
  would be tedious or obscure sequence and causation.

Do not prettify a display in a way that hides variation, uncertainty, missing
data, denominators, or negative results.

## Results

The Results section explains what the study found. It should complement the
tables and figures rather than read them aloud.

### Structure

- Organize results around the research questions or the sequence of tables and
  figures.
- Use informative subsection headings when the section is long or complex.
- State the high-level pattern, then direct readers to the supporting display.
- Highlight a small number of decision-relevant values, especially the primary
  comparison, effect size, uncertainty, and clinically or scientifically
  meaningful quantities.
- Report negative and null findings, not only favorable results.
- If a control group exists, make the treatment-versus-control comparison easy
  to find.

### Boundaries

- Put what was done and why in Methods.
- Put what was found in Results.
- Put what the findings mean in Discussion.

Do not justify a statistical model in Results if the justification belongs in
Methods. Do not interpret mechanisms or implications as though the data
directly established them.

### Tense and voice

Use past tense for completed measurements and analyses: `participants
reported`, `we found`, `the model estimated`. Use present tense for what a
display or the paper currently shows: `Figure 2 shows`, `the data suggest`.
Prefer active voice, but vary subjects among participants, measurements,
models, groups, and researchers rather than starting every sentence with `we`.

Reserve `significant` for statistical significance when ambiguity is possible.

## Methods

Methods should provide a clear, reproducible account of what was done while
making the procedure easy to navigate.

Ask whether the section answers the relevant versions of:

- Who or what was studied?
- What materials, interventions, instruments, and measures were used?
- When and where did procedures occur?
- How were variables obtained and data analyzed?
- Why were non-obvious design or analytic decisions made?

Include exact information necessary for replication, such as eligibility,
sampling, assignment, doses, timing, materials, measurement definitions,
instrumentation, preprocessing, missing-data handling, and analysis. Include
ethics approval, consent, registration, or animal-welfare information when
required.

Use informative subsections. Cite a complete and accessible prior method when
repetition would not help, but describe modifications and details needed to
reproduce this study. Use a table or diagram for complex workflows, dosing,
timelines, or participant flow.

Past tense usually describes completed procedures. Passive voice is acceptable
when the procedure deserves emphasis, but active voice may better expose who
made a choice. Jargon is acceptable when it is the precise language of the
method and the expected reader needs it.

## Introduction

The Introduction is a focused argument for the study, not an exhaustive review
of the field. The course describes a cone that narrows quickly:

1. What is known?
2. What remains unknown, inconsistent, limited, or contested?
3. What specific question, hypothesis, or aim does this study address?
4. How does the approach fill the identified gap?

Use an explicit purpose statement such as `We asked whether`, `We aimed to`, or
`We hypothesized that` when the venue permits it. Summarize bodies of prior work
at a high level instead of narrating one paper at a time. Include only
background that helps readers understand the exact question.

The course describes two to five short paragraphs, commonly about three, as a
useful biomedical norm. Treat that as a shape, not a universal length limit.
Write for the broadest plausible journal audience. Do not reveal detailed
results or implications before the question has been established unless the
genre or venue specifically calls for that structure.

## Discussion

The Discussion inverts the Introduction's cone. It begins with the answer and
broadens toward interpretation and implications.

### Recommended progression

1. State the primary finding as the answer to the stated question.
2. Add important secondary findings.
3. Explain how the findings fit, extend, or conflict with prior evidence.
4. Discuss plausible mechanisms while labeling inference and speculation.
5. Address study-specific strengths and limitations.
6. Identify unanswered questions and appropriate next work.
7. State the implications and a clear final take-home message.

Start and end with the main finding when that repetition helps readers retain
it. Do not open with limitations. Do not discuss variables the study did not
measure as though they were findings.

### Limitations

Anticipate the most serious informed criticism. Name the limitation, explain
the direction or kind of bias it could introduce when known, describe relevant
mitigation or sensitivity analyses, and state what remains unresolved. Avoid a
generic list that could be pasted into any paper.

### Interpretation

Keep conclusions close to the data. Distinguish observation from mechanism,
exploration from confirmation, surrogate from clinical outcome, within-sample
inference from generalization, and statistical from practical importance.

Use past tense for completed study details and prior studies. Use present tense
for current interpretation: `these findings suggest`, `one explanation is`.

## Abstract

Write the abstract from the finalized manuscript and analysis. It must stand
alone and match the full paper exactly.

Include, as space and venue permit:

1. one concise sentence of context;
2. the explicit question, objective, or hypothesis;
3. a minimal overview of design, setting, participants, and analysis;
4. the principal results with a few essential numbers, effect sizes, and
   uncertainty measures;
5. a conclusion that directly answers the question; and
6. a carefully calibrated implication or recommendation.

Do not reuse an early conference abstract without rebuilding it against the
final data. Check every number, outcome, sample size, and conclusion against the
main text and displays. Follow structured headings and word limits exactly.

## Narrative review

A narrative review synthesizes recent primary literature around a narrow,
explicit theme.

- Define the question, scope, audience, time span, and organizing thesis.
- Search systematically enough for the review's claim of coverage; do not call
  a search exhaustive unless it was designed and documented as such.
- Extract notes and citations into an organized road map before drafting.
- Organize by concepts, mechanisms, controversies, methods, populations, or
  another logic useful to readers, not by a procession of author names.
- State what is known, uncertain, contradictory, and missing.
- Compare study designs and evidence strength rather than counting papers as
  equal votes.
- End with a synthesis and specific, evidence-based research priorities.
- Write the abstract last.

For systematic or scoping reviews, use the relevant dedicated methodology and
reporting guidance; this narrative-review template is not a substitute.

## Grant proposal and Specific Aims

Current funder instructions override all defaults. Treat the Specific Aims page
as the proposal's conceptual road map and executive summary.

It should answer:

1. Why is the problem important?
2. What knowledge gap or unmet need blocks progress?
3. What is the long-term goal and the objective of this proposal?
4. What testable central hypothesis or organizing premise guides the work?
5. What specific aims and approaches address the gap?
6. What outcomes and payoff will move the field forward?

The overall objective should remain meaningful regardless of whether the
hypothesis is supported. Align gap, objective, hypothesis, aims, approach, and
payoff. Make dependencies and alternatives clear; an aim should not collapse
merely because an earlier aim gives an unexpected result.

For the full research plan, use the aims as a road map. Connect significance,
preliminary evidence, approach, rigor, feasibility, timeline, expected
outcomes, risks, alternatives, and future directions. Obtain feedback from
readers inside and outside the niche, state what type of feedback is needed,
and allow time to check that every application component tells the same story.

Do not replace honest uncertainty with unsupported certainty merely because a
proposal is persuasive writing.

## Protocol

A protocol is prospective and operational. State the question, rationale,
objectives, design, setting, eligibility, interventions or exposures, outcomes,
sample-size rationale, allocation and blinding where relevant, analysis plan,
missing-data strategy, ethics, monitoring, dissemination, and deviations
process. Use the current protocol reporting guideline for the study type.

Write planned actions in future or protocol-appropriate tense. Do not write as
though results already exist.

## Figure legends

A legend should let the figure stand alone. Include:

- a concise title that states the figure's point;
- what each panel, symbol, color, line, error bar, and abbreviation means;
- essential sample sizes, units, normalization, and statistical tests;
- what the error bars or intervals represent; and
- enough experimental context to interpret the display.

Do not smuggle interpretation into the legend if it belongs in Results or
Discussion.

## Posters and scientific presentations

Choose one principal message. Build the poster or talk around a sequence the
audience can follow: problem, gap, approach, evidence, answer, implication.

- Use declarative headings when they help readers recover the message quickly.
- Prefer visual evidence to dense prose.
- Make axes, units, denominators, sample sizes, and uncertainty visible.
- Avoid copying manuscript paragraphs onto slides or poster panels.
- Give technical detail in proportion to audience and available time.
- End with the finding and its calibrated implication, not a generic `Thank
  you` or `Future work` screen alone.

## Public-facing science writing

The underlying prose principles remain the same, but information order and
detail change.

### Six course-derived moves

1. Start with the take-home message.
2. Recognize and remove jargon, including ordinary words used in specialist
   ways.
3. Unpack the science from first principles.
4. Prioritize details rather than transferring the whole paper.
5. Get to the point quickly and trust the reader to make small inferences.
6. Tell a truthful story with people, concrete action, description, tension, or
   discovery when the material supports it.

Translate unfamiliar units for the audience while preserving the original
quantity. Explain analogies explicitly enough that readers know where the
comparison stops.

### Risk and numbers

- Prefer absolute risk or absolute change over relative risk alone.
- Give a common denominator and time frame: `8 more cases per 10,000 people per
  year` is more transparent than `a 26% increase` by itself.
- Present baseline and comparison risks together.
- Use whole-number frequencies when they aid comprehension.
- Keep the denominator consistent across comparisons.
- Do not hide population impact merely because individual risk is small, or
  exaggerate individual risk because a relative change is large.

### Science news story

A conventional structure is:

- a brief lead that captures attention and the heart of the story;
- a `nut graf` that tells readers what happened and why it matters;
- an early quote that supplies context, significance, or a human voice;
- a body that explains the question, evidence, findings, caveats, and outside
  perspective; and
- a satisfying ending or `kicker`, often circling back to the lead.

Quotes must remain faithful to what the speaker meant. Lightly remove verbal
filler only when the edit does not alter tone or meaning. Never manufacture a
quote.

## Peer-review report

Critique the work rather than the authors. Begin with a one-paragraph overview
of the paper and its contribution, then identify strengths and major concerns
before minor points.

- Make comments specific and actionable.
- Explain why a problem affects validity, interpretation, reproducibility, or
  readership.
- Avoid lectures, insults, vague generalizations, and exhaustive copyediting.
- Do not reveal an acceptance or rejection recommendation in comments intended
  for authors when the journal separates that recommendation.
- Put sensitive ethical concerns in the confidential editor channel when the
  venue provides one.
- Maintain manuscript confidentiality and follow the journal's AI-use policy.

## Response to reviewers

Respond point by point, respectfully and specifically. Quote or label each
comment, state the action taken, and identify the exact revised location. When
declining a suggestion, explain the scientific reason and consider revising the
text if the reviewer's misunderstanding exposed an ambiguity. Small harmless
changes are usually more efficient to make than to contest.

## Recommendation letters and personal statements

For recommendation letters, disclose how the writer knows the candidate and
for how long. Support judgments with specific, memorable evidence relevant to
the opportunity. Do not invent experiences, rankings, or praise. The letter
must represent the recommender's independent judgment; asking a candidate to
ghostwrite it undermines that purpose.

For personal statements, reveal authentic motivation and judgment rather than
repeating the CV. Use a concrete story when one genuinely explains the
applicant's trajectory. Do not fabricate adversity, emotion, accomplishment,
or future intent. A strong opening and a closing that returns to the central
thread can give the statement coherence.
